Current 01Shoegaze

The return of distortion as a language.

Shoegaze is also part of the return of guitars. This is not about bands covering The Jesus and Mary Chain or Sonic Youth, but about a new generation reclaiming their way of building sound: layers of distortion, fuzz, reverb, pedals and guitars that create texture as much as riffs. The modified Jazzmasters and alternate tunings explored by Thurston Moore and Lee Ranaldo are part of that lineage. Today those references reappear in new songs, often crossing into grunge, noise rock and alternative metal — evidence of a scene worth following.

Current 02New Grunge

Is a new grunge emerging?

A rougher guitar language is resurfacing: distortion, noise and references to Nirvana and Soundgarden are appearing in new bands. Some call it new grunge; I am interested in why this sound feels relevant again.

Musical trends can behave like a spiral: sounds return, but in a different time, generation and social context.

There is a sociological precedent for thinking about cultural trends as cycles rather than straight lines. Georg Simmel’s theory of fashion ↗ described cultural change through the tension between imitation and differentiation: forms spread, lose their distinction and make room for new ones. Later fashion-cycle research likewise treats styles as recurring processes of adoption and decline. My spiral is not Simmel’s model; it is my way of visualizing how a musical language can return without returning to exactly the same cultural point.

Transmission happens through unexpected routes. Matt Reeves’ 2022 film The Batman used Nirvana’s "Something in the Way" and placed the song before people who may never have listened to Nevermind. It also happens far more intimately: my children listen to Nirvana through me. Those discovering these records today are reaching an age at which they can form bands and transform influence into music of their own.

There is another intersection in what I hear emerging. Grunge and nu metal were recognizable, distinct scenes. Now some young bands take elements from both, together with shoegaze and noise, without worrying too much about respecting those boundaries. I have perceived that energy in Argentina, Mexico, New York and Berlin. I want to attach concrete names to that observation and test how far it extends.

There is also a generational question. I think of listeners now between 35 and 50 who did not fully connect with some of the dominant music of the last decade. The phrase "not everything is lost" appears when people talk about new guitar bands. But it would be a mistake to believe this music belongs only to those who remember the nineties: many of its creators and listeners are discovering those sounds for the first time.

It is worth remembering how grunge was initially received. To some people accustomed to the virtuosity of seventies and eighties rock, those songs could seem too simple and their voices too imperfect. What looked like a limitation was, for others, a direct way of saying something they needed to hear. That tension between musical expectations and the need for expression interests me more than nostalgia.

That is why I would focus on bands arriving at this sound organically. I want to find honest propositions, listen to what they have to say and observe how audiences respond. If we give them room to grow, the rawness, simplicity and abrasion that make this music valuable should remain intact. I believe society is ready to hear them.

Current 03Berlin Clubs & Techno

When club music recovers voice, identity and punk tension.

I am interested in a Berlin electronic underground where techno is no longer only functional club music. Vocals, EBM, industrial pressure and punk attitude are creating artists with recognizable identities, memorable songs and physical live propositions. The opportunity lies in projects capable of carrying that intensity beyond the club without losing the friction that made them relevant there.

Rick Rubin is a defining reference for how I think about creative and artistic direction. What I admire is his visceral, intuitive approach: listening deeply, recognizing what matters and helping each project find its own identity.

For me, his genius lies in the direction he gives a project beyond technical execution — the sensitivity to know what to protect, what to question and what to strip away. That is the creative leadership I aspire to bring to artists, music and every project I build.
